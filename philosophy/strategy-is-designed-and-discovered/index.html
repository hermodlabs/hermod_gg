A philosophy of intention and possibility

A team begins with an intention, but declaring a strategy does not make the composition capable of executing it. Every decision changes what the team can support, what else becomes possible, and which missing capability constrains several futures at once.

Our positionIntent gives the draft direction. Composition reveals what has actually become possible.
A declaration is a beginning

Names do not create capabilities.

A team can declare that it is building Protect Hypercarry. The declaration matters: it identifies what the team values, which kind of fight it expects, and how later choices should be judged. But the name itself does not produce frontline stability, access denial, carry protection, sustained damage, or conversion.

As champions are selected, the composition develops a concrete structure. Some choices strengthen the intended plan. Others produce capabilities that fit a different plan more strongly. Still others reveal that one missing job prevents several otherwise plausible directions from becoming usable.

A strategy is not made real by the name we give it. It becomes real through the relationships our decisions can actually support.

Alignment

Strategic Drift is a difference, not a verdict.

Drift appears when current support no longer resembles the declared structure as strongly as another direction does. The difference deserves explanation before criticism.

01Accidental drift

The language stayed while the structure changed.

The team continues speaking as though it is building protection while repeatedly selecting access and catch contributions.

02Adaptive drift

The team deliberately responds to new evidence.

The opponent makes the original plan unattractive, or a stronger opportunity appears, so the team changes direction on purpose.

03Apparent drift

The model may be incomplete.

A missing capability, condition, or realization rule may cause the model to underestimate how the selected champions support the intention.

Drift does not mean “bad draft.”

It says that intended meaning and current support differ. The team may recommit, adapt, or challenge the model’s account.

The deeper claim

Strategic possibility is shaped not only by what the composition possesses, but by which absence prevents several otherwise plausible relationships from becoming usable.

A bottleneck must earn the name

The most common missing capability is not automatically the most important.

01Recurrence

Does it affect several possibilities?

The missing capability appears across multiple relevant structures.

02Marginal impact

Would supplying it materially help?

Adding the capability improves or completes the affected possibilities.

03Feasibility

Can a remaining decision provide it?

A role-valid, available, and realistically usable provider remains possible.

04Distinctness

Are the unlocked futures meaningfully different?

The count is not inflated by several renamed versions of one pathway.

05Relevance

Do the possibilities matter here?

The objective, opponent, players, and current situation make them worth considering.

06Residual constraints

Would another absence still block them?

Supplying this capability does not leave every pathway equally unrealizable for another reason.

If no remaining decision can supply Reliable Access Denial, it may remain an important structural constraint—but it is no longer an actionable draft requirement.

What these ideas must not become

Diagnostics should create better questions, not silent commands.

Do not moralize drift

A difference from the initial intention may represent intelligent adaptation rather than failure or lack of discipline.

Do not overclaim emergence

Weak overlap with an alternate pathway is not a coherent strategic possibility, and structural support is not adoption.

Do not count bottlenecks blindly

Repeated absence matters only when the affected futures are relevant, distinct, feasible, and materially improved.

Do not treat intention as sacred

The first plan can be mistaken. It remains a comparison point, not an authority that later evidence must obey.

Do not hide model error

Apparent drift or missing support may reveal incomplete data, an overly broad condition, or a disputed strategic boundary.

Do not decide meaning automatically

The model may expose recurring structure. Players and communities establish whether it represents a meaningful strategy.

Say what changed

Replace a verdict with an inspectable strategic explanation.

Too simple

“This draft failed to build Protect Hypercarry.”

→
More useful

“The draft still has Jinx’s sustained conversion, but its selected champions supply target access, isolation, and immediate follow-up more strongly than independent protection. Pick & Collapse has become a plausible alternative, while Reliable Access Denial remains the highest-leverage missing job for protection-oriented directions.”

The second statement can be challenged one relationship at a time. It also gives the team three legitimate responses: strengthen the original intention, adopt the discovered possibility, or dispute the model’s interpretation.

Our position

Strategy is designed, discovered, constrained, and revised.

A team begins with an intention. Every decision then changes what the composition can support. Some choices preserve the intended structure. Some create another coherent possibility. Some reveal a missing capability that limits several futures at once.

The purpose of naming alignment, discovery, and constraint is not to replace judgment. It is to show players what changed so they can decide deliberately what the strategy should become.

Intent gives us somewhere to begin. Evidence gives us a reason to continue, adapt, or change direction.
