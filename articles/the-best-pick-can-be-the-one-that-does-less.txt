The Best Pick Can Be the One That Does Less
League of Legends drafting encourages a very natural instinct:
Take the pick that gives you the most value right now.
If one champion gives you stronger lane pressure, better teamfighting, more reliable engage, and better scaling than another, the decision seems obvious.
Choose the stronger champion.
But that logic can fail in a sequential draft.
Because a pick does not only determine what your team has.
It also determines what your team can still become.
Sometimes the champion that contributes less immediately preserves several strong future possibilities.
Sometimes the champion that looks strongest right now quietly commits the rest of the draft to solving the problems it creates.
And sometimes:
The best pick is the one that does less now because it leaves you able to do more later.
That is the strategic value of optionality.
Immediate Value Is Only Part of Draft Value
Suppose Blue is choosing between two champions.
Champion A strongly supports the team's current front-to-back plan.
Champion B supports that plan less strongly, but also leaves open:
dive;
counter-engage;
pick;
split pressure.
If we score only the current composition, Champion A wins.
Imagine:
VA=10V_A=10
and:
VB=7.V_B=7.
A simple optimizer concludes:
Pick A.
But the draft is not over.
There are still:
enemy picks;
enemy bans;
unresolved roles;
matchup information;
champion-pool constraints;
future strategic choices.
Champion B may contain something Champion A does not:
The right to wait before deciding what kind of composition this will become.
That right has value.
Drafting Is a Sequential Decision Problem
You do not choose five champions simultaneously.
You make one decision.
Then the opponent makes one.
Then the available world changes.
You learn something.
Another champion disappears.
A flex pick may become fixed.
A counter becomes visible.
A strategy becomes more or less attractive.
Then you choose again.
That means the value of preserving future choices can be substantial.
A pick that is slightly weaker in the current state may become stronger when evaluated across the range of future states that could still occur.
So instead of thinking:
Pick Value=Immediate Contribution\text{Pick Value}=\text{Immediate Contribution}
we need something closer to:
Pick Value=Immediate Contribution+Future Option Value.\text{Pick Value} = \text{Immediate Contribution} + \text{Future Option Value}.
The second term is easy to ignore because it is not yet visible on the scoreboard.
But during draft, it can be decisive.
Flex Picks Are the Obvious Example
Suppose Gragas can plausibly remain Top or Jungle.
If you pick him early, you have not only secured Gragas.
You have preserved ambiguity.
That ambiguity may allow you to wait before deciding:
where your frontline comes from;
which matchup you are willing to reveal;
whether Top becomes carry-oriented;
whether Jungle becomes utility-oriented;
how the opponent should allocate its counters.
The value of the flex is therefore not just:
Gragas can play two roles.
Its deeper value is:
The opponent must reason across multiple futures while you retain the right to choose later.
That is option value.
The pick may even be slightly weaker than another champion in one role.
But if it preserves enough future structure, it can still be strategically superior.
Optionality Is Not the Same as Vagueness
This distinction matters.
A composition is not strategically flexible merely because nobody knows what it is doing.
That is confusion.
Useful optionality means:
Several coherent future pathways remain available.
Suppose after three picks Blue can still plausibly complete:
Front-to-Back;
Engage & Follow-through;
Pick & Collapse.
That is meaningful optionality.
Now imagine another draft where Blue has five individually flexible champions but no clear pathway among them.
That is not strategic flexibility.
That is unresolved structure.
Optionality is valuable only when the available branches are themselves viable.
The Difference Between “Can” and “Could Still”
This is a useful way to think about it.
Ask two different questions.
What can our composition do now?
This measures current coverage.
What could our composition still become?
This measures future optionality.
Those are not the same thing.
A highly specialized draft might score extremely well on the first question and poorly on the second.
A flexible partial draft might look less complete right now but preserve several powerful completions.
Neither is universally better.
The choice depends on how much uncertainty remains.
Optionality Is More Valuable When Information Is Missing
Imagine you know almost everything about the opposing draft.
All five enemy champions are visible.
Their roles are clear.
Their intended strategy is obvious.
In that situation, preserving flexibility may not matter very much.
You have the information.
Commit.
Now imagine B1.
You know almost nothing.
The opponent has not revealed:
strategy;
important roles;
access mechanisms;
damage profile;
scaling;
preferred fight structure.
Here, preserving options can be much more valuable.
This gives us a general rule:
The value of optionality tends to rise with unresolved uncertainty.
The less you know about the future, the more valuable it can be to retain the ability to adapt to it.
A Pick Can Be Strong and Still Commit Too Early
Suppose Blue begins with Jinx.
That creates a powerful strategic asset.
But it may also increase future demand for:
protection;
frontline;
safe attack windows;
structured fight setup.
Now suppose Blue immediately follows with Lulu.
The Jinx pathway becomes stronger.
But Blue has also revealed even more.
The composition is increasingly committed to protecting sustained damage.
That might be excellent.
But the relevant question is no longer:
Is Jinx + Lulu strong?
It clearly can be.
The question is:
Was this the right moment to commit so much of the draft to that structure?
Perhaps yes.
Perhaps the team has deep supporting champion pools.
Perhaps the opponent has already revealed enough.
Perhaps the pathway is so strong that concealment no longer matters.
But perhaps Blue could have selected a champion that supported Jinx while preserving another plausible strategic direction.
Then the cost of the stronger immediate pairing becomes more visible.
Commitment Has Opportunity Cost
Every time you strengthen one pathway, you may weaken another.
Suppose a pick produces:
+3+3
value for Front-to-Back.
That sounds good.
But imagine the same pick also makes Dive and Split Pressure effectively impossible.
The true change is not:
+3.+3.
It is:
+3 to one pathway−the value of the pathways you gave up.+3\ \text{to one pathway} - \text{the value of the pathways you gave up}.
Those abandoned possibilities are an opportunity cost.
You did not merely gain something.
You stopped being able to become something else.
That is why two picks with similar immediate contribution can have very different strategic value.
The Best Pick Can Preserve a Branch
Imagine Blue has:
Top: unknown
Jungle: Maokai
Mid: Orianna
ADC: Jinx
Support: unknown
The team currently has the beginnings of a strong structured teamfight composition.
Now Blue considers two Top champions.
Option A: Ornn
Ornn strongly reinforces:
frontline;
initiation;
front-to-back structure.
The plan becomes extremely coherent.
Option B: Gragas
Gragas may provide somewhat less pure front-to-back strength.
But depending on role and later picks, he might preserve:
frontline;
disengagement;
pick;
flexible role assignment;
different fight geometries.
If Blue already knows Red's entire plan, Ornn may be clearly preferable.
But if major uncertainties remain, Gragas may buy something valuable:
time.
Not game time.
Decision time.
Blue can wait for more information before deciding exactly what kind of team it wants to become.
Preserving a Pathway Has Value Even If You Never Use It
This can feel strange.
Suppose Gragas preserves a Pick & Collapse pathway.
Blue eventually does not use it.
Was that optionality wasted?
Not necessarily.
The possibility itself may have influenced the opponent.
If Red has to respect both:
structured front-to-back;
pick threat;
it may have to draft differently.
It may delay a role reveal.
It may spend a ban differently.
It may preserve a defensive option it would otherwise abandon.
So a branch can create strategic value even if it is never chosen.
The opponent must spend reasoning and draft resources accounting for it.
This is similar to how a threat in gameplay can matter without being executed.
The option changes behavior.
Optionality Can Have Information Value
A flexible pick also lets you learn.
Suppose you select a champion who fits several possible compositions.
The opponent responds.
Now you know more.
Maybe Red reveals heavy disengagement.
The Dive pathway becomes less attractive.
Maybe Red reveals low range.
Poke becomes more appealing.
Maybe Red commits to strong front-to-back.
Split Pressure becomes more attractive.
The flexible pick allowed you to postpone commitment until after receiving that information.
That means optionality can function as a form of information acquisition:
Preserve options now so later decisions can be conditioned on better information.
This is especially important early in draft.
But Optionality Is Not Free
If optionality were always good, every team would just maximize flexibility forever.
That would also be wrong.
Preserving choices has costs.
A flexible champion may:
be weaker in the expected matchup;
provide less immediate synergy;
have lower player comfort;
fail to fully solve an urgent structural need;
delay necessary commitment;
create execution complexity.
So we can think of the tradeoff as:
Net Optionality Value=Future Branch Value+Information Value+Concealment Value−Immediate Performance Cost−Complexity Cost.\text{Net Optionality Value} = \text{Future Branch Value} + \text{Information Value} + \text{Concealment Value} - \text{Immediate Performance Cost} - \text{Complexity Cost}.
Sometimes the number is positive.
Sometimes it is not.
The point is not:
Always draft flexibly.
The point is:
Treat preserved possibilities as a real asset instead of pretending only immediate contribution matters.
There Is Such a Thing as Too Much Optionality
A team can also preserve options past the point where commitment becomes necessary.
Suppose by B4 your composition still has:
no reliable initiation;
no clear damage profile;
uncertain lane assignments;
multiple possible pathways.
You might describe that as flexible.
But perhaps what you really have is indecision.
Eventually the team must convert possibilities into an executable plan.
Optionality is useful because it lets you choose later.
It is not useful if you never choose.
This creates another strategic problem:
When should a draft stop preserving options and start completing a pathway?
That is a timing question.
Early draft may reward flexibility.
Late draft increasingly rewards coherence.
The Value of Optionality Changes Over Time
Imagine you have five draft decisions remaining.
Preserving three future pathways can be valuable.
Now imagine only one pick remains.
If none of those pathways is complete, having three possibilities is not impressive.
It may be a liability.
So option value often decays as the draft progresses.
Early:
preserve useful possibilities.
Middle:
learn from opponent responses.
Late:
convert preserved options into a coherent plan.
That gives us a useful draft rhythm:
Preserve
→ Observe
→ Commit
→ Complete
A strong draft does not maximize flexibility at every stage.
It manages the timing of commitment.
The Opponent Can Attack Your Optionality
Optionality can also be denied.
Suppose a champion preserves both:
Front-to-Back;
Dive.
But both future pathways depend on only three remaining support champions.
The opponent bans two.
Suddenly your apparent flexibility collapses.
This means option value should not be counted only by the number of pathways still technically available.
You also need to ask:
How robust are those options to future denial?
A pathway supported by ten realistic champion combinations is very different from one supported by a single fragile branch.
This is the difference between:
possible
and:
safely preservable.
Fake Optionality
This creates another common mistake.
Suppose your draft technically supports three pathways.
But:
Pathway A requires a champion nobody on the roster plays.
Pathway B depends on a role already heavily constrained.
Pathway C requires a champion likely to be banned.
You do not really have three options.
You have one realistic option and two theoretical ones.
Good optionality must be realizable.
That means it depends on:
champion pools;
role assignments;
player comfort;
remaining bans;
opponent incentives.
A possibility that your team cannot actually execute should not be counted as meaningful flexibility.
Optionality Is Team-Specific
This is why the same flex pick can have different value for different teams.
Suppose Gragas can flex Top/Jungle.
Team A has:
excellent Gragas Top;
excellent Gragas Jungle;
deep replacement pools in both roles.
For Team A, the flex is real.
Team B's Jungler cannot play Gragas comfortably.
Now the theoretical flex is mostly cosmetic.
The champion still has multiple professional role assignments.
But the team's actual option set does not.
This gives us an important principle:
Strategic optionality belongs to the roster, not just the champion.
A flex pick is only flexible if your actors can realize the branches.
Optionality Also Exists Above Roles
Flexibility is often discussed only as:
Champion can play Top or Mid.
But strategic optionality is broader.
A champion might preserve multiple:
pathways;
resource structures;
fight styles;
lane allocations;
objective plans.
Consider a champion who can participate effectively in both:
Engage & Follow-through
and:
Pick & Collapse.
Even if the champion has only one role, that can still be strategically flexible.
The most interesting question is therefore not:
How many roles can this champion play?
It is:
How many coherent futures does this pick preserve?
Diversification Versus Completion
Now imagine the draft already has one nearly complete pathway.
You have:
Access
→ Control
→ Damage
→ ?
→ Conversion
One candidate closes the final missing requirement.
Another creates an entirely new secondary pathway.
Which should you choose?
The closer creates completion value.
The diversifier creates option value.
Again, neither is universally better.
If the primary pathway is fragile or easy to counter, diversification may be valuable.
If the primary pathway is extremely strong and difficult to disrupt, completion may dominate.
This is a genuine strategic tradeoff:
Do we make our best plan stronger, or do we create another way to win?
That is a much better question than:
Which champion has the highest score?
Redundancy Is Another Form of Option Value
Suppose you already have initiation.
Do you need more?
Maybe not.
But if your only initiation source is highly conditional, a second independent initiation route could be extremely valuable.
The second champion may appear to “do less” because they do not add a completely new capability.
But they may create a backup pathway.
For example:
Primary initiation fails
→ secondary initiation remains available
That increases robustness.
So not all apparently redundant picks are wasteful.
The question is:
Does the additional capability create an independent route, or does it merely duplicate the same dependency?
True redundancy preserves options after failure.
Fake redundancy collapses with the same bottleneck.
Optionality Matters Because Opponents Adapt
If the opponent were passive, you could simply choose the strongest known plan.
But the opponent is actively trying to make your plan worse.
That makes adaptability valuable.
Suppose you reveal one rigid pathway early.
Red can spend its remaining draft deliberately attacking it.
But if Blue preserves two or three credible structures, Red has a harder problem.
Whatever it attacks, Blue may still have somewhere else to go.
This gives optionality an adversarial value:
A strategy with several credible futures is harder to counter efficiently.
The opponent may be forced to hedge.
And hedging is expensive.
A Pick Can Make the Opponent Pay for Uncertainty
This is perhaps the strongest way to think about flex value.
A flexible pick does not merely preserve options for you.
It can impose uncertainty costs on the opponent.
Suppose Red cannot tell whether a champion will:
Top or Jungle;
engage or disengage;
support Dive or Front-to-Back.
Red may now need to select a response that survives several interpretations.
That can force Red away from a highly specialized counter.
So the value of your option is partly:
your adaptability+their uncertainty cost.\text{your adaptability} + \text{their uncertainty cost}.
That is much richer than:
flex pick good.
When the Stronger Pick Really Is Better
None of this means you should fetishize optionality.
Sometimes Champion A simply should be picked.
Suppose:
you already know the enemy composition;
the team has chosen its strategic objective;
the pathway is clear;
Champion A completes it;
the opponent has few ways to disrupt it;
your players can execute it comfortably.
Then preserving theoretical alternatives may simply reduce the strength of the plan you actually intend to play.
At that point:
Commitment is not a flaw.
It is the point of drafting.
The mistake is not commitment.
The mistake is committing before the value of commitment exceeds the value of waiting.
A Better Pick Evaluation
Instead of asking only:
What does this champion add?
ask:
1. What does this champion add immediately?
Capabilities, matchups, synergy, resources.
2. Which pathways become stronger?
What does the pick help complete?
3. Which pathways disappear?
What are you giving up?
4. Which roles or responsibilities become fixed?
How much ambiguity remains?
5. What new information will arrive before the next commitment?
Can waiting improve the next decision?
6. How easily can the opponent attack the options you preserved?
Are they real or fragile?
7. Can your roster actually execute every preserved branch?
Theoretical flexibility is not enough.
8. What is the cost of waiting?
Are you leaving an urgent structural problem unresolved?
Those questions turn optionality into something you can reason about rather than something you simply praise.
The Best Pick Can Be the One That Does Less
Suppose one champion:
gives more immediate damage;
more lane pressure;
more obvious synergy.
And another:
gives slightly less now;
preserves two roles;
keeps three pathways alive;
reveals less to the opponent;
allows the team to learn from the next enemy pick before committing.
The second champion may look weaker in a static comparison.
But the draft is not static.
It is a sequence of decisions under uncertainty.
That changes the meaning of value.
The best pick is not always the champion that contributes the most to the current composition.
Sometimes it is the champion that preserves the most valuable future decisions.
That is why:
Doing less now can be strategically better if it keeps you able to do more later.
The important question is not merely:
What does this pick give us?
It is also:
What does this pick leave us free to become?
