A Strategy Is More Than Its Best Line

A Hermod design position

A composition should not be understood only by the strongest sequence it can execute. We also need to ask what happens when that sequence is interrupted, a required condition disappears, or the team must choose another way forward.

Our positionStrategic resilience comes from both independence within a pathway and meaningful alternatives between pathways.
The problem with the best line

“We can do this” is not the same as “we can keep doing this when resisted.”

A coverage score can show that the selected champions supply the jobs in a pathway. That is useful, but incomplete. The same composition may rely on one champion to establish several steps, one condition to make every contribution usable, or one sequence the opponent can recognize and deny.

Strategy is played against resistance. The enemy moves, holds cooldowns, changes formation, denies vision, and refuses the fight we designed in our heads. A useful account of a composition therefore needs to describe both its preferred line and its ability to survive the loss of that line.

The strength of a plan includes what remains possible after the obvious plan stops working.

How players use the distinction

Make the fallback part of the plan before the plan fails.

The report is valuable when it improves a decision, a call, or a review—not when it merely gives the composition another label.

1
During draft

Find the irreplaceable job

Ask whether one champion supplies several stages that fail together. If access and control both depend on Rakan, compare the next pick by whether it adds an independent entrance—not simply more damage after a perfect engage.

2
Before the fight

Name the condition that protects the line

If Poppy can deny the critical entry, the useful call is concrete: track her position, avoid entering through the threatened route, and wait until the relevant denial tool cannot protect the target.

3
When resistance appears

Change pathways instead of repeating failure

If safe access never appears, stop forcing the fragile sequence. Move to a retained pathway the composition actually supports, such as protecting Jinx and controlling space in a front-to-back fight.

4
During review

Separate execution from structural dependence

Ask whether the player misplayed, the required condition never appeared, or the draft supplied no independent alternative. These are different failures and should produce different changes.

A necessary guardrail

More redundancy and more branches are not automatically better.

A team can reasonably trade flexibility for focused strength. The preferred strategy may fit the enemy, player familiarity may favor specialization, or adding redundancy may leave another essential job uncovered. Branch loss is not a moral failure, and provider concentration is not proof of a bad draft.

The report should expose the trade
  • What became stronger?
  • What became dependent?
  • Which meaningful alternatives disappeared?
  • Was the exchange deliberate?

Fragility and branch loss are costs to understand, not mistakes to prohibit.

Say it like a team

Replace a vague instruction with a shared strategic boundary.

Before

“We need Rakan to engage.”

→
After

“Our primary pathway depends on Rakan finding access. If Poppy holds her denial, we protect Jinx, control space, and stop forcing that entrance.”

The second statement does more than sound analytical. It identifies the preferred pathway, its critical dependency, the enemy condition that matters, and the branch the team will use if that condition remains unfavorable.

Our position

Make dependence and lost choice visible.

Hermod should not tell a team that specialization is wrong. It should show when a composition’s apparent strength depends on one provider or one path, identify the alternatives that remain, and let players decide whether the exchange fits their intent.

That boundary keeps the model descriptive where it can calculate structure and leaves the strategic commitment where it belongs: with the people playing the game.

A resilient strategy knows its best line, what can break it, and what the team will do next.
