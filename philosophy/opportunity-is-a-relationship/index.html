A Hermod design position

A weakness is not automatically a strategy. A strength is not automatically relevant. A strategic opportunity appears when something about the opponent connects to something your team can actually do—under conditions that make the connection usable.

Our positionOpportunity belongs to the relationship between an opponent, an allied pathway, and the situation—not to either side in isolation.
The distinction

Seeing a weakness and possessing an answer are different facts.

An enemy champion can be immobile whether or not your team can reach them. Your composition can have excellent isolation whether or not isolation matters against this opponent. Both observations may be true while no practical opportunity connects them.

Strategy begins when we connect those facts. We must be able to say what characteristic matters, which pathway can exploit it, which capabilities supply that pathway, and what conditions must hold. Without that connection, “weakness” is only a description and “strength” is only unused capacity.

A weakness becomes a strategic opportunity when this team has a credible way to exploit it in this situation.

The common reasoning mistake

Three claims are often collapsed into one.

1Observation

“The enemy carry has low mobility.”

This describes a characteristic attributed to an opponent.

≠
2Capability

“Our composition has strong damage.”

This describes something the allied team may be able to supply.

≠
3Strategic conclusion

“Therefore, we should isolate that carry.”

This requires a relationship the first two claims do not establish by themselves.

The missing questions

Can we establish access? Can we prevent escape or reinforcement? Can we concentrate pressure in time? Can we convert the removal? What can the enemy do to interrupt that sequence?

A concrete League example

Low mobility matters differently to different compositions.

The enemy characteristic stays the same. The strategic conclusion changes when the allied pathway changes.

How a player can use the distinction

Turn the report into a better question at each moment.

The result is useful when it changes what the team asks, watches, or prepares. It should not replace the player’s read of the game.

1
During the draft

Do we have a route, or only a target we would like to reach?

In the first fragment, adding more damage does not repair the missing relationship. The team should examine an access or isolation provider, choose a different pathway, or accept that this enemy characteristic is not currently actionable.

2
Before the fight

Name the conditions that make the opportunity usable.

For the connected example, the team can agree that Jinx must be separated from protection, the approach must avoid known vision, and Poppy’s position or dash-denial cooldown must be accounted for before committing.

3
While playing

Watch for the relationship—not merely the champion.

Jinx appearing on screen is not the signal. The signal is Jinx occupying reachable space while allied access and follow-up are available and the enemy’s counter-pressure cannot close the route.

4
During review

Locate which part of the argument failed.

Was the enemy characteristic wrong? Did the draft lack a capability? Did geometry or timing invalidate a condition? Did Poppy interrupt access? Or was the route available and the execution unsuccessful? Those are different lessons.

Counterfactual example

What changes if Braum becomes Vi?

Before: the team has follow-up damage but no modeled Target Access. Jinx’s low mobility remains an observation without a supported isolation route.

→

After: Vi adds conditional Target Access. The report can form an evidence-backed relationship, but should also expose the access condition and Poppy’s ability to pressure it.

The useful explanation is not “Vi is better.” It is “Vi changes which strategic relationship this composition can currently support.”

Why we chose this boundary

It makes recommendations slower—and more trustworthy.

We could label every immobile champion a target and every access champion a counter. That would produce more answers. It would also produce answers that hide the reasoning players actually need.

01

It prevents category mistakes

A characteristic, a capability, a pathway, and a recommendation are different kinds of claim. Keeping them separate prevents one fact from impersonating a complete argument.

02

It preserves directionality

We ask who can exploit what, through which pathway. “A is vulnerable to B” is more useful than a universal label saying “A is weak.”

03

It makes disagreement precise

People can challenge the enemy characteristic, the pathway connection, the capability evidence, or the conditions instead of arguing over an unexplained recommendation.

04

It keeps game state relevant

A structurally relevant opportunity may still be unusable because vision, cooldowns, formation, range, timing, or player execution do not support it now.

A necessary warning

“Curated” does not mean “objectively true.”

When Hermod says a characteristic is curated, it means someone deliberately asserted it and made it inspectable. It does not mean the characteristic is permanent, context-free, or beyond disagreement.

  • Patch dependenceA balance change can alter mobility, protection dependence, effective range, or the conditions around an ability.
  • Definition dependenceTwo analysts may use “low mobility” differently: no dash, limited escape, long cooldown, or dependence on allied movement.
  • Situation dependenceItems, cooldowns, formation, allied protection, and terrain can change whether the characteristic matters.
  • Model incompletenessIf we did not encode a characteristic or relationship, the report cannot use it. Absence from the model is not evidence of absence from the game.
Our obligation

Curated claims should carry definitions, provenance, scope, and revision history. Where evidence is absent or conditions are unresolved, the product should show uncertainty rather than filling the gap with confidence.

How confidence should grow

Relevance is not realization.

The relationship becomes more useful as additional evidence arrives, but the stages should remain distinguishable.

1Relevant relationship

A modeled pathway is conceptually connected to the enemy characteristic.

2Potential opportunity

The selected champions provide evidence for some or all of the pathway’s capabilities.

3Conditioned opportunity

Important range, timing, geometry, information, and target-state conditions appear available.

4Realized opportunity

Observed game evidence shows that the relationship was successfully exploited.

Hermod should never promote a relationship merely because it is attractive. Unknown conditions remain unknown. An interpreted opportunity remains a hypothesis until stronger evidence supports it.

How the position shapes the product

This philosophy creates visible constraints.

The report must show both sides

Enemy characteristics and allied pathway support appear together so the conclusion can be traced.

Empty states must explain missing evidence

“No opportunity detected” means the model cannot support the relationship—not that the game contains no opportunity.

Conditions must qualify support

Potential, conditional, and confirmed supply cannot be collapsed into the same kind of coverage.

Counter-pressure must remain visible

An exploitable weakness can coexist with enemy tools that interrupt the pathway used to exploit it.

Profiles must become editable

Communities should be able to disagree with our defaults and test what follows from their own declared assumptions.

The final decision remains human

The system can discover and explain a possibility. It cannot silently decide target priority or issue the team’s call.

A position, not a loyalty test

You can disagree with this boundary.

Someone may reasonably argue that recognizing a vulnerability is already strategically valuable, especially while draft slots remain. Another community may define opportunity more broadly than we do.

Our answer is not that those meanings are forbidden. Our answer is that separating a vulnerability from an actionable opportunity produces recommendations that are easier to inspect, challenge, and revise. This is the boundary Hermod uses, and we should be clear about it.

We are not asking people to accept the conclusion. We are showing the reasoning so they can decide whether the conclusion deserves acceptance.
