A philosophy of commitment and adaptation

A team needs enough commitment to coordinate its decisions and enough flexibility to respond when the game refuses to follow the script. Strategy lives between those two failures.

Our positionChoose what matters. Commit to a route. Preserve another way.
The tension

A strategy must direct action without pretending to control the future.

Drafting asks a team to make commitments before it knows exactly how the match will unfold. Players need a shared answer to what matters and how they intend to create it. Without that answer, champion choices accumulate without becoming a composition.

But the opponent chooses too. Conditions change. A lane loses priority, an initiation angle disappears, or the enemy refuses the fight the composition was built to win. A strategy that recognizes only one acceptable sequence becomes brittle the moment reality disagrees.

Priority creates coordination. Alternatives create resilience.

Two strategic failures

A team can be aimless or trapped.

More options are not always better, and stronger commitment is not always wiser.

Too little commitment

Five picks. No shared theory.

Every champion appears individually reasonable, but the team has not established which result matters most, which sequence should organize the composition, or which tradeoffs it will accept.

Options without direction do not coordinate action.
Too much commitment

One script. No way to adapt.

The team treats one objective and one pathway as mandatory. When the enemy denies a critical condition, the composition repeatedly forces a sequence that no longer has a usable opening.

Direction without alternatives becomes helplessness.
Organize what matters

Two objectives should not mean two equal wishes.

A hierarchy helps the team resolve tradeoffs. A list merely records that several outcomes would be nice.

1Primary objective

What result organizes our decisions?

Critical Asset Preservation gives the team a default answer when priorities compete: keeping the carry able to contribute matters most.

qualified by
2Secondary concern

What must remain important while we pursue it?

Space & Information Control prevents protection from becoming purely passive. The team also wants to shape approaches and see threats before they reach the carry.

The secondary objective is not another item in a shopping list.

It can constrain how the primary objective is pursued, preserve a necessary complement, or make a deliberate tradeoff visible. When the two conflict, their ordering tells the team what normally wins.

Organize how it happens

Different pathways should have different jobs.

Preferred, alternative, and fallback do not mean first, second, and third favorite. They describe different relationships to uncertainty.

01Preferred pathway

What do we intend to make happen?

The default theory of action when the important conditions are available.

Shape space → deny access → preserve carry → sustain damage → convert
02Alternative pathway

What different route can we deliberately choose?

A coherent possibility that changes how value is created rather than merely renaming the preferred sequence.

Establish vision → isolate target → collapse → convert
03Fallback pathway

What remains when the preferred route is denied?

A route whose purpose is continuity. It need not be equally strong to keep the composition from becoming strategically inert.

Control range → apply pressure → disengage → reset
A strategy is a policy, not a script

Decide what to do when the expected situation does not appear.

A script says what must happen. A policy connects different conditions to deliberate responses.

IfSafe formation and controlled approaches are available→

Use the preferred protection-and-conversion pathway.

IfThe enemy separates a vulnerable target→

Use the alternative isolation pathway rather than forcing the expected fight.

IfReliable access never becomes available→

Use the fallback pressure-and-reset route while preserving the composition.

Adaptation is not abandonment.

Changing routes can preserve the objective more faithfully than repeating the original sequence after its enabling conditions have disappeared.

Make each choice answerable

A champion pick should reveal what it commits and what it preserves.

The hierarchy turns “Is this champion good?” into questions that can be examined against the team’s theory.

01

Does this strengthen the preferred route?

Name the required job and the evidence connecting the champion to it.

02

Does it undermine the secondary concern?

A pick can improve the main pathway while violating the way the team intended to pursue its objective.

03

Does it preserve a meaningfully different alternative?

Several differently named routes are not genuine options if they all depend on the same unavailable condition.

04

What remains if the critical sequence fails?

A fallback makes the consequence of resistance explicit before the team is forced to improvise.

05

Is the focused gain worth the branches it closes?

Commitment can be correct. The important thing is to make the exchange deliberately rather than discovering it afterward.

Flexibility has a cost

The goal is not to preserve every possible strategy.

A composition that tries to retain every objective and every pathway may lack enough concentration to execute any of them well. Optionality requires resources: champion slots, role eligibility, player practice, communication, and cognitive attention.

A focused composition may knowingly close several branches because the preferred plan fits the opponent and the players. A flexible composition may accept less concentrated strength because uncertainty makes adaptation more valuable.

The purpose of alternatives is not to avoid commitment. It is to keep commitment from becoming blindness.

Commitments are hypotheses

A declared strategy earns revision, not loyalty.

The objective hierarchy and pathway portfolio establish a starting theory. Champion evidence and enemy choices may support it, qualify it, or make another direction more coherent.

How this position shapes Hermod

The numbers are prompts, not doctrine.

Hermod invites teams to name a primary objective and one secondary concern, then distinguish a preferred pathway from an alternative and fallback. This structure is meant to encourage priority, contingency, and adaptation—not to declare that every real strategy naturally contains exactly two objectives and three pathways.

The secondary objective, alternative pathway, and fallback may be left empty. Inventing a commitment merely to complete a form produces less understanding, not more.

A useful default asks a better question.

It should never force the player to pretend they have an answer.

Our position

Commit enough to act together. Preserve enough to respond.

Strategic direction tells the team which result and pathway organize its choices. Strategic flexibility tells the team what remains when the expected opening disappears.

Neither eliminates judgment. Together they make commitment, tradeoff, and adaptation easier to explain.

A strategy should give the team a direction without demanding that reality follow the script.
