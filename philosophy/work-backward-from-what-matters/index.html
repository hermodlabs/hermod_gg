A philosophy of outcomes, constraints, and intervention

A champion action is not strategically valuable by itself. Its value depends on the result you need, the sequence that could produce it, and the requirement currently preventing that sequence from working.

Hermod’s core decision promiseWork backward from a strategic result. Find the required job limiting its pathway. Show which available choice changes that constraint.
Start with purpose

“What can this champion do?” is only half a question.

Darius can pull, slow, damage, and threaten an execution. Those facts matter, but they do not tell us whether Darius solves this team’s problem. We first need to ask what result the team could pursue and what must happen for that result to become possible.

This reverses the usual direction of champion advice. We do not begin with a champion and decorate the choice with reasons afterward. We begin with a desired result, trace the work it requires, and inspect whether the choice changes the limiting work.

A choice matters when it changes the system that must produce the result.

The problem this position addresses

Players are surrounded by answers that begin too late.

A tier list begins with a ranking. A champion guide begins with a champion. A post-game argument often begins with the action that looked wrong. By then, the purpose of the decision has already been assumed. “We needed a tank,” “we needed damage,” or “you should have engaged” can sound concrete while hiding the result that the advice was supposed to produce.

That hidden assumption matters because the same action can be correct for one purpose and wasteful for another. Walking into river can establish vision before an objective, create a flank, protect a teammate’s rotation, or simply expose the player. The motion is visible; its strategic function depends on what the team needs to happen next.

This is the need Hermod is trying to fill. We want to help a player move from “this champion does useful things” to “this choice changes the requirement that currently prevents a useful result.” That is a narrower claim than declaring a universally best pick. It is also a more useful claim when the player must decide what to do.

Purpose may be implicit, but it is never absent

Every recommendation optimizes for something.

An organized team may state its objective explicitly. Five Solo Queue players usually will not. That does not free an analysis from needing an objective; it only means the analysis must distinguish a declared plan from a plausible one. If a visible composition can create access, isolate a target, and convert the removal into an objective, we may examine that route. We should not pretend the five players agreed to it.

Without a result to work toward, “better” collapses unrelated values into one word. Safer lane execution, stronger first contact, more independent pressure, better carry protection, and greater strategic flexibility are not interchangeable. A player can reasonably choose among them, but only after the difference is visible.

Software should name the purpose used to judge a choice, using the context the player has already supplied. When intent is unknown, the conclusion should remain conditional: if this is the result you want, this is the work that currently limits it.

One decision, several scales

Even a micro choice inherits a purpose.

The levels do not compete. Higher levels provide direction; lower levels reveal whether that direction is actually executable.

If players cannot supply a required action under real conditions, the pathway is not realizable. Micro evidence can force us to revise a meso sequence, a macro plan, or even an assumption about the meta.

What the diagram is reinforcing

A lane decision cannot be evaluated in a strategic vacuum.

Suppose Maya can push her lane. Mechanically, that may be true. Whether she should push depends on what the priority is for. If her jungler needs first movement toward Grubs, the push may supply access to the river. If the team is conceding the objective and the enemy jungler can punish the extension, holding the wave may preserve more value. The micro fact stays the same while its strategic use changes.

The opposite direction matters just as much. A coach can describe a beautiful macro plan, but if its first step requires a player to execute an unfamiliar champion under an unreliable timing window, the plan is not ready merely because it is coherent on paper. The lower level supplies evidence about whether the higher-level idea can survive contact with the game.

This is why Hermod separates structural possibility from realizability. We need both the reason for an action and evidence that the action can be supplied under the conditions in which it matters.

Conditions and enemy pressure qualify every link. A tool that exists on a champion sheet may still be out of range, mistimed, blocked by geometry, dependent on follow-through, or vulnerable to an enemy response.

Why constraints matter

More capability is not the same as more progress.

A pathway behaves less like a bag of strengths and more like a sequence of dependent work. If the team can reach a target but cannot survive the entry, adding another way to reach the target may increase activity without making the sequence more executable. If the team can engage and damage but cannot turn the result into durable value, additional damage may decorate the pathway while conversion still limits it.

We call the weakest required job the constraint because it limits the readiness of the whole route. Sometimes several jobs share that position. Those are co-constraints: fixing only one may leave the pathway floor exactly where it was. This protects the analysis from rewarding a champion simply for having many mapped tools.

“Weakest” is evaluated in a declared order. We compare evidence grade first, then the number of best-grade providers, then the least unresolved-condition burden on one of those providers, and finally modeled enemy pressure. The order matters: adding three conditional providers can make a route less fragile, but it cannot turn conditional evidence into confirmed evidence.

The practical promise is diagnostic. Instead of saying “pick more engage,” the system should be able to say which modeled result is under consideration, which job holds it back, whether a candidate changes that job, and what becomes limiting afterward.

How to interpret the example

“Constraint unchanged” describes Darius’s effect on this requirement.

The example begins with Breach and Remove because that is the result being tested. It then identifies five required jobs tied at the lowest modeled supply grade. Darius is introduced as an intervention. The relevant question is whether his modeled contributions improve any of those weakest jobs under the available conditions.

In this comparison, they do not. The honest conclusion is therefore limited: Darius does not relieve the constraint on this pathway. He may improve lane pressure, punish a different enemy characteristic, reinforce a job that already has supply, or support another pathway. Those possibilities require their own traces.

This distinction is central to trust. A negative result should tell the player exactly what failed to connect. It should never smuggle “does not solve this requirement” into “is a bad champion.”

When five jobs are tied at the same lowest readiness, improving only one can leave the pathway floor unchanged. This reveals that the system has more than one limiting requirement.

Why the answer must be recalculated

Solving a problem changes which problem matters next.

In the second example, conversion begins far below the other stages. Raising it from 24 to 61 is meaningful because it increases the pathway’s weakest supply. But the intervention does not finish the analysis. Exploit now becomes the lowest stage, so the next choice should be evaluated against that newly limiting requirement.

This is why a reusable strategy model cannot be a static checklist. Every pick changes the available providers, remaining roles, activation conditions, enemy pressure, and alternative routes. A recommendation that was sensible before the pick can become redundant after it.

For a player, this creates a disciplined question: what does the team need now? For Hermod, it creates an obligation: calculate the consequence of the choice, expose the new limiting job, and allow the explanation to change with the evidence.

A repeatable way to reason

Make the constraint visible before recommending the intervention.

  1. 1
    Name the result.

    What game-state change are we trying to produce?

  2. 2
    Trace its requirements.

    Which pathway and ordered jobs could produce that result?

  3. 3
    Locate the floor.

    Which required job or co-constraints have the weakest condition-aware supply?

  4. 4
    Compare an intervention.

    Does the champion improve that job, merely add unrelated value, or introduce another dependency?

  5. 5
    Recalculate the system.

    If the constraint moves, what becomes limiting next?

What the conclusion can honestly claim

A constraint trace explains the modeled dependency.

It can show
  • One modeled strategic result and its required pathway
  • The weakest condition-aware required stages
  • Whether a candidate improves those stages
  • The constraint that remains after the change
It cannot prove
  • The universally best champion
  • That strangers intend to play this pathway
  • The winning draft or correct live-game action
  • Patch strength, lane state, or evidence absent from the model
Why we make the reasoning visible

The player should be able to disagree at the right place.

A recommendation can be wrong because the modeled result is irrelevant, the pathway is poorly described, a required job is missing, a champion mapping is incomplete, a condition is unrealistic, or the player values a different tradeoff. Those are different disagreements. Showing the reasoning lets the player identify which one they actually have.

That matters more than defending the output. Hermod’s defaults should remain revisable. Community knowledge changes, patches alter what champions can supply, and players discover uses that the current model does not contain. A visible constraint trace turns disagreement into information that can improve the model.

We are not asking the player to surrender judgment to a system. We are promising to do the tracing work clearly enough that the player can use, question, or reject the conclusion for a reason.

Our position

Improve the requirement that limits the plan.

A champion may do many impressive things without changing the job that holds a strategy back. Another may add only one relevant tool and transform what the composition can plausibly execute. The difference becomes visible only when we work backward from what matters.

Result → requirements → constraint → intervention → new constraint.
