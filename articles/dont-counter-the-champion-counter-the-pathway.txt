# Counter the Pathway That Makes Their Draft Work

The opponent is building around Jinx, and your team is considering a dive answer. Before spending the remaining picks, ask: **which part of their plan can we disrupt affordably, and what advantage can we take afterward?**

This article shows how to compare two attacks on the same opponent, prepare for a response, and retain a way for your own team to progress. The examples use preparation assumptions about the rosters and game state.

## Map the Opponent's Intended Fight

Suppose Blue wants a front-to-back fight at Dragon. It plans to push mid, enter river first, establish protection around Jinx, and make Red approach. Jinx then gets an attack window, and Blue converts a favorable fight into Dragon.

Begin with a fair account of the plan's strength. Assume Blue's players can hold their formation and protect Jinx against a single frontal attacker. A direct dive into that setup will require coordinated access, enough damage, and a way to survive the response.

Red has another possible target: Blue needs time and lane movement to establish that formation. If Red can contest mid and enter river first, Blue must approach through space that Red controls.

The pathway makes both candidates visible. One attacks the protected carry; the other attacks the setup that creates her useful attack time.

## Compare the Cost of Each Attack

Assume Red has already selected a mid laner that can contest the wave and a jungler that can help secure the river approach. Its players are comfortable coordinating that movement. A reliable dive would require both remaining picks to provide access and follow-up, leaving little room to repair other needs.

Under those conditions, contesting setup is the cheaper starting plan. Red already owns much of the required structure and can use a remaining pick to improve its entrance control or follow-up.

That decision still needs an executable sequence. Red prepares the mid wave, moves its nearby players together, and controls an entrance before Blue arrives. If Blue enters in a broken formation, Red looks for a short attack on the exposed entrant, forcing health or cooldowns before Jinx can establish a comfortable damage position.

The expected gain is a retreat that gives Red time to take Dragon. Red should start it only while its health, wave state, and control of the remaining approaches support that conversion.

This completes the comparison: the setup attack costs fewer new draft resources, uses practiced coordination, and leads to an objective through a named sequence.

## Check Blue's Response

Blue can change the conditions. It might reinforce mid, use a different entrance, arrive earlier at a cost elsewhere, or decline the contest.

Red therefore needs evidence at each transition. Can the mid wave still be contested? Can teammates move together? Does the controlled entrance matter, or can Blue approach safely from another side? An opponent who refuses a bad entry has not handed Red a kill.

If Blue gives up the contest, Red can take Dragon when its control makes that safe. If Blue changes entrances, Red must preserve a formation that covers the actual approach. Chasing the original trap after Blue bypasses it would abandon the reason the plan worked.

The same checks apply to a dive alternative. If Blue uses protection to stop the first attacker, Red needs a credible second action and enough resources left to complete it. A neutral lane matchup can be acceptable when the pick reliably performs that team job.

## Use Bans to Shape the Response

Evaluate bans against the counterplan's purpose. Which available champions help Blue recover the setup or create a safe entry that Red is trying to deny?

Suppose Blue has three realistic providers for a needed opening in its remaining roles. Removing one leaves two. Red should inspect the lineups those two can still complete before spending another ban on the same job.

A useful ban may remove an efficient one-slot solution and force Blue to spend two picks restoring its structure. That benefit disappears if Blue has already secured another provider or can switch to an equally usable route.

During preparation, rehearse the ban in its actual decision window and let Blue respond with a pick or substitute. Prefer the ban that weakens the response Red most needs to control at an acceptable opportunity cost. Describe a route as closed only when no viable completion remains under the stated pool and availability assumptions.

## Change the Mid-Lane Assumption

Now suppose Blue's next pick gives it a mid-lane setup that Red cannot reliably contest. Red's preferred route loses its first step. Adding more entrance control would do little if Red cannot arrive in position to use it.

Assume this is an early Dragon Red can afford to concede, and its Top pick already provides credible pressure on the opposite side. Red can revise the plan: avoid an expensive late river entry, use the movement elsewhere to take an available structure, and reset before Blue's next rotation.

That trade needs a worthwhile return within the available time. If Red cannot obtain one, calling it side pressure does not make the concession profitable. Red may need to accept a smaller loss and use a remaining pick to improve the next contest.

The choice changed because Blue removed the affordable setup attack. Red now judges a map trade by its own conversion and timing. A dive becomes attractive only if the completed roster can fund it more reliably than these alternatives.

## Choose an Attack and Its Next Branch

1. Which step in their route can we interrupt with the tools we can afford?
2. What sequence turns that interruption into our own objective or map advantage?
3. How can they respond, and which part of our sequence would that change?
4. What evidence would make us choose a different attack or accept a trade?
