Five Good Champions Can Still Make a Bad Team

Turn a desired team result into player assignments, check each transition, and revise the plan when its starting conditions change.

HermodLabs / 12 min read / Articles

In this field guide

  1. Build a Pathway to the Objective
  2. Assign Each Step to a Player
  3. Find the Missing Link
  4. Follow the Fight Through to Dragon
  5. Change the Arrival Order
  6. Turn the Lineup into Assignments

Your team has damage, crowd control, and protection, yet its objective fights keep falling apart. At Dragon, those tools have an obvious purpose: control could hold an enemy in reach, damage could force that enemy away, and protection could keep your damage dealer attacking through the response.

Imagine Gnar catches an enemy beyond a river entrance. Jinx has damage available, but reaching that target would require crossing an enemy-controlled choke. Lulu has followed Gnar; assume she is now too far from Jinx to protect her against a threat from the other side. Gnar's control expires while Jinx waits for a usable approach. The team has spent its opening without bringing its damage and protection into the same fight.

All three tools were present. Their targets, positions, and timing failed to connect. Before changing a champion, ask: what sequence should turn these five picks into a won fight and an objective? This article shows how to turn a desired result into player assignments and find a missing connection. We will build one plan, check what each step needs, and change the conditions to see when the plan stops working.

Build a Pathway to the Objective

Consider this hypothetical roster:

  • Top: Gnar
  • Jungle: Graves
  • Mid: Viktor
  • Bottom: Jinx
  • Support: Lulu

Assume the players are comfortable on these champions and want to contest Dragon. Their desired result is to force the opponent away, keep enough health to remain in river, and take the objective. An ability is a champion's particular mechanism, such as Lulu's Help, Pix! A basic capability names the reusable mechanical tool it supplies. Help, Pix! can shield an allied target, so that use supplies Shielding.

A strategic capability describes a team-level job, such as Ally Protection or Zone Control. Lulu's shielding can contribute to Ally Protection when she can reach Jinx and the shield helps Jinx withstand the relevant threat. If Lulu is too far away, having the shielding tool recorded on the roster leaves that protection job unresolved at the moment it matters.

A pathway connects the required strategic jobs into a route toward the desired result. Here, the team wants to establish a formation, control enemy access, give Jinx time to attack, and use the resulting advantage to take Dragon. Each ability is a candidate way to perform part of that work under particular conditions.

Each transition creates a requirement. The formation must let Jinx reach a useful target. Protection must last through her attack window. After the fight, the team needs enough health and control of the approach to finish the objective safely.

Assign Each Step to a Player

Start with a favorable setup: the team can move first, and Gnar can arrive with a useful transformation timing. Gnar holds threatening space at the front. Viktor controls an entrance, while Graves helps cover the approach and adds damage. Lulu stays in a position to protect Jinx. Jinx attacks the targets that this formation makes safe to reach.

These assignments explain why the champions help one another. Gnar's threat discourages a free approach; Viktor makes repositioning costly; Lulu preserves the attack time that Jinx needs. Graves can use the space without requiring the entire team to follow a separate attack.

The plan also assigns resources. Before Dragon, Viktor needs a wave state that lets him move. Gnar needs time to prepare and join. Lulu's attention belongs near Jinx during the contest, so the team must arrange vision without assuming she can also escort a distant invade.

The team can now make a preparation decision: organize the approach around arriving together with those conditions ready. A player who needs extra time should call it before teammates spend cooldowns or cross into river.

Find the Missing Link

Now inspect the action that actually starts the fight. Gnar's strongest initiation needs suitable timing, terrain, access, and allies ready to follow. Viktor and Jinx can punish an opponent who enters their space, but that does little to force an enemy standing beyond their reach. If the opponent must approach Dragon, its movement can supply the opening. The team holds its formation and uses that approach to begin dealing damage.

If the opponent can stay at range, the sequence may stall before Jinx gets a useful target. Counting several crowd-control abilities would conceal this problem: some only extend an opening that someone else must create. For this roster, the review exposes a conditional connection between holding space and starting a favorable fight. The preparation question is whether the team can arrange a contest that makes the opponent approach.

Follow the Fight Through to Dragon

Suppose the opponent enters, Gnar creates an opening, and the team forces two enemies to retreat. The next call still needs evidence. If Jinx can keep attacking Dragon, Graves can remain with her, and the others can cover the remaining entrances, the team has a conversion plan. If the fight leaves the team low on health or scattered, starting Dragon could surrender the advantage.

Assign that decision too: identify who checks the remaining threats and who calls whether to finish, reset, or take a smaller gain. A successful opening is only one stage of the intended result.

Change the Arrival Order

Change one assumption: the opponent gets into river first. Gnar's timing is still useful, but our formation is no longer established, and the enemy has little reason to walk into Viktor's control.

The earlier plan no longer supports an immediate forced entry. For an early Dragon the team can afford to concede, a reset or available map trade may be preferable. If the objective must be contested, preparation needs a workable access sequence before calling this lineup suitable for that situation. The assessment has changed because a named step lost its starting conditions. That tells the team what to practice, arrange earlier, or address in a future draft.

Turn the Lineup into Assignments

  1. What fight, objective, or map advantage do we want to produce?
  2. Which steps get us there, and who performs each one?
  3. What must each player receive before their step can work?
  4. If one condition changes, where does the sequence break and what do we do next?
