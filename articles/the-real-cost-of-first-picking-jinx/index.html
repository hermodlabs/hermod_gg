The Real Cost of First-Picking Jinx

Judge an early anchor by the supporting jobs, realistic completed lineups, and fallback your roster can afford.

HermodLabs / 4 min read / Articles

In this field guide

  1. What Jinx Needs to Function
  2. Sketch Two Realistic Completions
  3. Estimate the Support Cost
  4. Compression Preserves Draft Space
  5. Change the Supporting Pool
  6. Price the Anchor Before Taking It

Jinx is available early, and your team wants her sustained damage and objective conversion. Can the remaining picks support her at a cost the team is willing to pay? This article shows how to evaluate an early anchor through realistic completed lineups. We will identify Jinx's supporting requirements, compare two ways to fill them, and test the decision after an important option disappears.

What Jinx Needs to Function

Jinx needs useful attack time. In a structured front-to-back fight, that can require someone to hold space, control enemy access, and protect her position. The supporting requirements depend on the opponent: a team facing direct backline access may need more peel, while another may struggle to start a fight at all.

For this exercise, assume the intended route needs frontline, a workable opening, protection, and wave control. Orianna supplies mid-lane wave control and follow-up in the rehearsed completions. We hold her availability fixed here; a branch that removes her needs its own completion check. The Top, Jungle, and Support choices must make the rest affordable.

Choosing Jinx creates draft debt: the supporting work left for later picks. A useful first-pick review names that work before promising that the team can complete it.

Sketch Two Realistic Completions

Assume this roster has practiced the following two lineups and accepts their matchups in the scenario:

  • Ornn Top,
  • Graves Jungle,
  • Orianna Mid,
  • Jinx Bottom,
  • Lulu Support.
  • Gnar Top,
  • Maokai Jungle,
  • Orianna Mid,
  • Jinx Bottom,
  • Janna Support.

In the first completion, Ornn supplies frontline and initiation, Lulu protects Jinx, and Graves receives room to contribute damage. In the second, Maokai takes more responsibility for frontline and the opening, Janna protects the formation, and Gnar joins around a prepared timing.

Both leave a credible way for Jinx to attack. Their costs differ. The second puts Jungle on setup duty and requires the team to coordinate Gnar's arrival. The first depends more heavily on securing Ornn to cover the front. These are illustrative assignments. A champion counts as a solution only if the appropriate player can use it, its role remains available, and the finished lineup can perform the intended sequence.

Estimate the Support Cost

The comparison can be organized as:

Working expressionAnchor value = Immediate value + Future synergy − Future support cost

This expression structures a judgment; it provides no numerical estimate by itself. For our team, support cost includes the Top and Support choices committed to enabling Jinx, the Jungle contribution each completion permits, and any matchups accepted to secure those picks. It also includes the practice and timing the completed plan requires.

Suppose the team prefers a damage-oriented jungler but can comfortably use Maokai when needed. It would choose the Ornn completion first and accept the Maokai completion if Ornn disappears. Jinx is a reasonable early anchor under those assumptions because the team can fund both its preferred structure and a usable fallback.

A roster with no practiced Maokai alternative would reach a different assessment. The same early carry would depend on one narrower supporting package.

Compression Preserves Draft Space

Ornn and Maokai each cover multiple requirements in these examples. This is compression: one pick supplies enough of several jobs to leave another slot available. If the replacement for Maokai supplies frontline but cannot create the opening this team needs, another role must finish that work. Support may then have to provide initiation while also preserving Jinx's attack time. The replacement's cost includes that new obligation.

Check the full assignment before treating two champions as substitutes. Covering several capability labels saves draft space only when the jobs can be delivered in the intended fight. In our completions, protection has a separate provider, so the initiator does not also have to remain beside Jinx throughout the opening.

Change the Supporting Pool

Run the same first-pick review with different availability. Ornn is already unavailable, and the opponent can secure Maokai before our next opportunity. Assume our preparation has found no acceptable replacement for his setup role in the second lineup. Jinx now carries a larger completion risk. Gnar and Graves remain available, but this team's intended forced opening is unfinished. Their individual strength does not establish an acceptable substitute lineup.

Under these assumptions, we would pass on early Jinx unless the team can validate another completion. If Jinx is already secured, we must reassess the fight route and the work the remaining slots can still perform. The important evidence is the set of acceptable finished teams. A long list of apparent protection or frontline champions can shrink quickly after accounting for bans, occupied roles, and player pools.

Price the Anchor Before Taking It

  1. Which supporting jobs does this anchor leave for our remaining picks?
  2. What preferred completion can our players execute, and what does it cost?
  3. If the opponent removes its key supporting pick, which complete lineup would we still accept?
  4. Is that remaining choice strong enough to justify the anchor now?
