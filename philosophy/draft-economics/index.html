A Hermod philosophy of commitments, scarcity, and revision

Every early pick changes the future work of the draft. It makes some plans easier to complete, makes other plans more expensive, and creates demand for particular strategic functions under particular conditions.

Our positionAn early commitment creates demand for the strategic functions needed to make it useful.
Why the opening matters

A pick spends more than a slot.

Imagine that a team opens with Jinx. The champion supplies a powerful sustained-damage payoff. That fact alone does not tell us what the draft should become. The team could build a protected-carry plan, use Jinx as one threat inside a front-to-back structure, or later add another damage engine and reorganize the composition.

The opening matters because each proposed use creates a different set of obligations. A protected-carry plan asks whether Jinx can receive usable attack windows. A front-to-back plan asks whether the team can hold a stable fight shape. A second-threat plan asks whether both carries can function without consuming the same protection, space, and attention.

The useful question asks: “What must this team still supply to make this proposed use of Jinx work?”

This is why Hermod treats an anchor as a contextual hypothesis. A champion may become a carry anchor, a structural anchor, or a pathway anchor in one draft and play a supporting role in another. Flexibility describes how much useful choice remains. Conditionality describes how much later evidence the hypothesis still needs.

Five commitments behind the analysis

Draft value lives in relationships.

These principles determine what the simulator counts, what it explains, and where it leaves the decision to the team.

01

Value is contextual

A champion becomes valuable through the result a team wants, the jobs already supplied, the roles still open, and the alternatives that remain playable.

02

Commitments create conditional demand

Securing a payoff mechanism changes what the team needs next. The opening creates future work only in relation to the plan being proposed.

03

Scarcity belongs to the team

A theoretical provider has little practical value when the role is occupied, the champion is outside the player pool, or its contribution depends on conditions the team cannot create.

04

Efficiency can concentrate dependence

One champion can efficiently solve several requirements. The same compression can make several parts of the plan fail together when that champion becomes unavailable.

05

Good drafting includes revision

Later evidence can reinforce the opening, add another payoff, share the organizing role, move the anchor, or reveal that the original interpretation should change.

The central tradeoff

Compression creates efficiency and concentration at the same time.

Efficiency

One pick solves several requirements.

The team spends fewer future slots on the work needed to complete the proposed pathway. More of the draft remains available for damage, protection, flexibility, or another plan.

⇄
Concentration

Several requirements depend on one pick.

A ban, opponent selection, role conflict, or execution failure can remove several pieces of the pathway together. Several apparent branches may share the same hidden dependency.

A compressed plan can be efficient and fragile. The decision requires visibility into both consequences.

Good drafting includes revision

Later picks can change what the anchor means.

Adding Azir after Jinx, Lulu, and Ornn introduces several possible interpretations. Hermod must inspect which strategically useful routes remain when one payoff cannot function and whether both payoffs share the same conditions.

Reinforcement

A later pick makes the proposed anchor's existing route easier or more reliable.

Diversification

A later pick establishes another strategically useful payoff or pathway.

Shared anchoring

Several contributions become jointly central to how the composition works.

Migration

A different contribution becomes the composition's main organizing center.

Reinterpretation

Later evidence changes our account of what the earlier choices were building.

How to use the idea

Compare commitments by the future work they create.

  1. 1
    Name the result.

    Choose the objective that gives the comparison a purpose.

  2. 2
    Propose an anchor.

    State what contribution might organize the plan and keep its status as a hypothesis.

  3. 3
    Trace the remaining jobs.

    Find role-valid, playable combinations that can complete the pathway.

  4. 4
    Remove an unsecured option.

    Recalculate completions, shared dependencies, role pressure, and alternate plans.

  5. 5
    Revise deliberately.

    Complete, substitute, diversify, or pivot according to the changed constraint.

The boundary of the claim

The simulator explains consequences under declared assumptions.

It can establish
  • Which pathway requirements remain
  • Which declared providers and role assignments can satisfy them
  • Whether several plans share one dependency
  • How an opponent denial changes feasible completions
The team still decides
  • Which objective deserves priority
  • Whether the proposed pathway fits the game
  • Whether the player-pool assumptions are accurate
  • Whether focused strength is worth reduced flexibility

When the declared pool cannot complete a pathway, the honest result is direct: no feasible completion was found under these assumptions. That result invites the team to change the pool, revise a mapping, or choose another plan.

Our position

A strong opening is one whose future cost the team understands.
