Find the Bottleneck in Your Draft

Find the step that limits a composition's progress and choose an improvement that addresses its cause.

HermodLabs / 3 min read / Articles

In this field guide

  1. Trace the Outcome Back Through the Fight
  2. Find the Stage That Limits Progress
  3. Compare Improvements at the Limiting Stage
  4. Change the Diagnosis When the Evidence Changes
  5. Choose the Next Improvement

Blue reaches Dragon first and lands its initiation, yet Jinx spends most of the fight retreating. Should the team add damage, change its formation, or improve the setup? This article shows how to locate the step limiting a plan and choose an improvement that addresses its cause.

That limiting step is a strategic bottleneck. The diagnosis needs evidence from the intended game state: a lineup's list of strengths gives little indication of where its execution will stall.

Trace the Outcome Back Through the Fight

Consider Ornn, Sejuani, Orianna, Jinx, and Lulu. Blue wants to win a structured fight and take Dragon. To do that, it needs early movement, useful objective position, control of enemy access, a safe attack window, damage, and enough resources to finish the objective.

Assume a review of this scenario shows Blue arriving first with healthy champions and usable cooldowns. Ornn and Sejuani reach their targets. Jinx can initially reach the fight, but an enemy access threat then forces her backward. Dragon remains available if Blue wins. This evidence narrows the investigation. Initial access is working, and conversion is still possible. Progress repeatedly stops when Jinx loses her attack window.

Find the Stage That Limits Progress

Several strengths depend on that window. The frontline buys time, initiation exposes targets, Orianna controls space, and Lulu protects Jinx. Their value falls when Jinx cannot use the time to attack. The dependency diagram shows why a team can appear well supplied while producing little damage. The shared condition needs attention even when every role is filled.

The cause still matters. In this example, assume the review shows an enemy approach left uncontrolled. Jinx retreats from that threat despite having targets in range. That supports a formation or defensive-control adjustment. A range problem would require a different response.

Compare Improvements at the Limiting Stage

The flow diagram assigns hypothetical capacities to the fight's stages. The attack window has the smallest capacity, so adding damage potential elsewhere produces limited benefit while that restriction remains. For illustration, suppose Blue currently gives Jinx two seconds of useful attack time. Compare changing a slot to add damage with rehearsing a formation that controls the approach and gives her six seconds. These times are hypothetical teaching values.

With the stated cause, prioritize the formation adjustment. It uses the existing lineup to make more of Jinx's damage available. Test it against the same enemy approach and watch whether the extra attack time produces a won fight and Dragon. A longer window alone is insufficient evidence that the full plan now works.

Change the Diagnosis When the Evidence Changes

Now suppose Blue already creates a long attack window and wins the fight, but neglected waves force it to recall before taking Dragon. More protection offers little help at the stage where this version stalls. Move the preparation priority to wave timing and the resources needed for conversion. The champions can stay the same while the limiting stage changes.

Use repeated examples from a comparable setup before making a broad draft change. First access, late arrival, different terrain, and the roster's practiced champion pool can each change which improvement is feasible. After any adjustment, trace the whole sequence again to see where progress stops next.

Choose the Next Improvement

  1. What outcome are we trying to produce, and where does the sequence repeatedly stall?
  2. What evidence explains that failure, and which feasible change addresses its cause?
  3. After that change, can the remaining stages turn the improvement into the intended outcome?
