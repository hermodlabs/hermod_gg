Five Good Champions Can Still Make a Bad Team
One of the easiest mistakes to make in League of Legends is assuming that a composition is good because every champion in it is good.
Imagine a team drafts five champions that are individually strong, comfortable for their players, and perfectly reasonable in isolation.
There may be no obvious troll pick. No losing lane was deliberately drafted. Every player can explain why their champion is useful.
And yet when the game reaches an important objective fight, something feels wrong.
The team cannot start the fight it wants.
The carry cannot safely deal damage.
Two champions want to play forward while the others want to play backward.
The jungler needs lane priority that the composition cannot consistently create.
Someone engages, but nobody can follow.
The team technically has everything a conventional checklist might ask for—damage, crowd control, frontline, mobility—but those pieces do not form a coherent way of winning.
The problem is simple:
A collection of good champions is not necessarily a good system.
That distinction is at the center of composition strategy.
Champions Have Capabilities. Teams Need Pathways.
A champion can provide useful things.
For example:
initiation,
sustained damage,
burst damage,
peel,
disengagement,
target access,
zone control,
wave control,
frontline durability.
But possessing capabilities is not enough.
Those capabilities have to combine into a sequence that actually produces something the team cares about.
Suppose a composition wants to win through a traditional front-to-back teamfight around a scaling ADC.
A simplified pathway might look like:
Establish a stable frontline
→ prevent enemy access
→ create a safe attack window
→ maintain sustained damage
→ convert the fight into an objective
Each step depends on something else being true.
The ADC's damage may be excellent, but that damage only matters if the ADC is allowed to attack.
The support may have exceptional peel, but the peel only matters if the team can remain organized enough to use it.
The frontline may absorb damage, but that is only strategically useful if the rest of the team can exploit the time it creates.
This is why composition analysis should not stop at:
What can these champions do?
The stronger question is:
Can what these champions do be assembled into a reliable pathway toward the team's objective?
The Missing-Link Problem
Consider a hypothetical composition:
Top: Gnar
Jungle: Graves
Mid: Viktor
ADC: Jinx
Support: Lulu
Every champion is individually defensible.
Gnar gives durability, disruption, and teamfight presence.
Graves gives damage, farming strength, and skirmishing power.
Viktor gives control and sustained damage.
Jinx gives enormous late-game damage and conversion.
Lulu gives protection and peel.
At first glance, the composition seems loaded.
It has scaling.
It has damage.
It has protection.
It has some frontline.
It has crowd control.
But ask a different question:
How does this team reliably start the fight it wants?
Now the answer becomes less obvious.
Gnar can engage, but his strongest initiation is conditional. He needs Mega Gnar, useful terrain, sufficient access, and teammates positioned to follow.
Lulu is primarily helping the team protect rather than force.
Viktor prefers enemies entering controlled space.
Jinx wants time and protection.
Graves often wants room to operate rather than being responsible for forcing the initial commitment.
Nothing about those champions is inherently bad.
But the composition may have a structural dependency:
A large portion of its teamfight plan depends on Gnar producing the right kind of initiation under the right conditions.
That is a much more important observation than saying:
“This composition has good scaling.”
The issue is not whether Gnar is good.
The issue is how much of the team's strategy depends on one conditional function.
The Same Capability Is Not the Same Pathway
Another common mistake is counting capabilities without asking how they interact.
Imagine a team has three champions with crowd control.
It is tempting to conclude:
We have plenty of engage.
But crowd control is not identical to initiation.
And initiation is not identical to reliable fight access.
Three champions may all have crowd control while requiring the fight to begin under completely different circumstances.
One may require the opponent to walk into range.
Another may require a flank.
Another may only become reliable after an ally has already created access.
The team does not necessarily have three independent ways to start a fight.
It may have:
one initiation pathway with three pieces inside it.
That distinction matters.
If all three champions depend on the same condition, the apparent redundancy can disappear.
For example:
Champion A requires enemy proximity.
Champion B follows Champion A.
Champion C follows Champion B.
It would be misleading to describe that as three independent engage sources.
Structurally, it may be closer to:
Access
→ Setup
→ Follow-through
Break the first step and the rest may become unavailable.
This is why counting features can create false confidence.
Five Champions Can Also Want Five Different Games
There is another way a composition can fail.
The champions may all be strong, but they may create conflicting demands.
Suppose:
Top wants to pressure a sidelane.
Jungle wants to invade with priority.
Mid needs time to scale.
ADC wants the team grouped around them.
Support wants to roam early.
Every individual preference can be sensible.
Together, they may create a resource problem.
Who gets jungle attention?
Who gets the safe waves?
Who gets vision support?
Who is supposed to play weakside?
Who must move first for objectives?
Who receives protection during fights?
League has limited resources:
gold,
experience,
lane priority,
map presence,
vision,
cooldowns,
attention,
draft slots,
time.
A composition therefore has a kind of resource topology.
Some plans distribute responsibility cleanly.
Others quietly ask several players to receive the same scarce resource.
A team can draft five individually powerful champions and discover that four of them all expect to be the center of the game.
That is not a champion-strength problem.
It is a systems problem.
A Composition Should Be Able to Answer “Then What?”
One useful way to test a draft is to keep asking:
Then what?
Suppose someone says:
We have strong engage.
Then what?
We start the fight.
Then what?
We burst a target.
Then what?
We win the fight.
That final jump may be hiding the entire problem.
Can the rest of the team reach the target?
Can they continue dealing damage after the first cooldowns?
Can the enemy disengage?
Does the engage leave your own carry exposed?
What if the target survives?
What happens if the opponent refuses the fight?
What happens after the kill?
Can you convert Baron, Dragon, a tower, or map control?
A coherent composition should have plausible answers across several transitions.
For example:
Find isolated target
→ establish access
→ constrain escape
→ concentrate damage
→ secure kill
→ convert numerical advantage
That is much more informative than:
“This is a pick comp.”
The label is useful, but the pathway explains how the strategy works.
Good Compositions Often Have Backup Routes
A strong composition also does not necessarily need one perfect plan.
In fact, having only one plan can make a draft brittle.
Imagine two compositions.
Composition A
It has an extremely powerful front-to-back teamfight.
But nearly everything depends on one champion successfully creating the first engagement.
If that champion is behind, denied access, or forced to use cooldowns defensively, the team's strategy deteriorates rapidly.
Composition B
Its primary teamfight is slightly weaker.
But it can also:
threaten picks,
disengage,
pressure a sidelane,
contest space around objectives.
Which composition is better?
There is no universal answer.
But Composition B possesses something strategically valuable:
Optionality.
It has multiple ways to turn the game into a favorable state.
That can make it more robust against uncertainty.
This becomes especially important during draft.
A champion may be slightly worse for the team's current plan while preserving several future plans.
Another champion may maximize the current plan but commit the remaining draft very heavily.
The better pick depends on how much you value immediate strength versus future flexibility.
That is a strategic tradeoff, not something a static champion score can settle.
Early Picks Create Obligations
This is why first picks deserve special attention.
Suppose a team first-picks Jinx.
The pick immediately supplies valuable things:
scaling,
sustained damage,
strong fight conversion.
But it may also create future demand for:
peel,
frontline,
protected attack windows,
reliable setup.
In other words, the team has not simply acquired Jinx.
It has also created a set of future obligations.
A useful way to think about an early pick is:
**Anchor value
= immediate value
future synergy
− future support cost**
That final term is easy to ignore.
A champion can be extremely powerful while still being expensive to build around.
If the remaining champion pool provides many cheap ways to satisfy those future requirements, the commitment may be excellent.
If the necessary supporting capabilities are scarce, heavily contested, role-constrained, or vulnerable to bans, the same first pick can become much more expensive.
This is one reason champion value cannot be completely separated from draft state.
The value of a pick depends partly on what it makes you need next.
The Opponent Gets a Vote
Composition analysis also becomes much more useful when the opponent is treated as an active participant.
Suppose your team has built a beautiful pathway:
Engage
→ lock down target
→ follow through
→ Jinx attack window
→ objective conversion
The obvious question is:
Does the pathway work?
The stronger question is:
Where would the opponent attack it?
Perhaps the enemy drafts disengagement.
Now your initiation may still occur, but follow-through becomes unreliable.
Perhaps they draft backline access.
Now Jinx's protected attack window becomes expensive to maintain.
Perhaps they avoid your teamfight entirely and create sidelane pressure.
Now your strongest pathway may be difficult to realize because the game refuses to enter the state your composition prefers.
This leads to an important principle:
A good team composition is not merely internally coherent. It must remain coherent after an opponent begins interfering with it.
That is a much higher standard.
There Is No Universal “Good Draft”
This also explains why draft grades can be misleading.
Suppose someone asks:
Is this composition an 8/10?
The question sounds precise, but precise relative to what?
A composition might be excellent if:
your players are comfortable on the champions,
the opponent must engage into you,
objectives force grouped fights,
your scaling arrives on time.
The same composition may be weak if:
your frontline player cannot reliably realize the required engagement,
the opponent can split the map,
your lanes cannot produce priority,
your primary damage source is easily accessed.
So instead of asking:
How good is this draft?
ask:
Under what conditions is this draft good?
And then:
Which of those conditions are fragile?
That transforms draft analysis from ranking into reasoning.
A Better Composition Checklist
The next time you look at five champions, don't start by asking whether each champion is strong.
Ask:
1. What is the team trying to accomplish?
Can you describe the strategic result clearly?
2. What pathway gets the team there?
What must happen first, second, and third?
3. Which capabilities does that pathway require?
Initiation? Access? Peel? Wave control? Damage? Disengagement?
4. Who supplies each capability?
And are those providers independent?
5. What conditions have to be true?
Does someone require a flank? Setup time? Lane priority? Frontline?
6. Where are the bottlenecks?
What single failure would disrupt several parts of the plan?
7. Are there competing resource demands?
Does everyone need the same gold, attention, or map priority?
8. What alternative pathways exist?
If Plan A becomes unavailable, what can the team do instead?
9. How does the opponent attack the structure?
Not just which champion counters which champion.
10. What would make your assessment change?
This may be the most important question of all.
A strategic judgment should have conditions under which it can be revised.
The Difference Between a Roster and a Composition
Five champions are a roster.
A composition is something more.
A composition contains relationships:
Who enables whom?
Who protects whom?
Who creates the conditions another champion needs?
Where do resources go?
What happens when the opponent interferes?
What does the team do if its first pathway fails?
Those relationships are what transform five champion selections into a strategy.
And that leads to a much more useful way to think about drafting:
The goal isn't to collect the five strongest pieces.
The goal is to construct a system whose pieces make one another strategically useful.
Sometimes that means taking the champion with the highest individual upside.
Sometimes it means taking the champion that fills the missing structural role.
Sometimes it means taking a seemingly weaker champion because they preserve two different ways to play the game.
And sometimes five very good champions still make a bad team.
Not because the players drafted bad champions.
Because they never finished building the system.
