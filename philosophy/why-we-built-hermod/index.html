Why we built hermod.gg

League of Legends does not suffer from a shortage of data or opinions. The missing step is often the one between knowing many facts and deciding what to do in this situation—and being able to explain why.

Our purposeTurn game knowledge into decisions players can explain, test, and revise—without asking them to surrender their judgment.
The need we see

The community has knowledge. Too often, it has nowhere durable to live.

Every day, players notice things that matter. A support player knows why a lane becomes unplayable after one cooldown is lost. A jungler understands that two forms of engage are not interchangeable. A coach sees that a composition can win a fight but cannot reliably reach the fight it wants. A theorycrafter recognizes that a patch changed more than damage—it changed which strategic branches remain possible.

That knowledge is real, but it is often trapped inside experience. It appears in a stream, a scrim conversation, a guide, a Discord argument, or the sentence “this comp just feels bad.” Then the context disappears. The next discussion starts again from champion names, tier lists, win rates, authority, and instinct.

We do not think players need another oracle telling them what is correct. We think they need better tools for making their reasons visible.

When reasons stay hidden, strategic disagreement easily becomes social disagreement. We defend the player, creator, rank, region, or style we identify with. A failure becomes somebody’s fault before it becomes a question. The loudest explanation can win even when nobody has defined what the composition was trying to accomplish.

Hermod exists to slow that moment down. What was the objective? What kind of plan did we choose? What jobs had to happen, and in what order? Which champion mechanics could supply those jobs? Under which conditions? What could the opponent interrupt? What did our decision make possible—and what did it close?

The missing layer

More data does not automatically produce a decision.

Players already have statistics, builds, rankings, matchup tables, guides, and opinions. Those sources can describe what is common or what happened. They cannot, by themselves, determine what this composition needs from this player against this opponent.

Available information

“This champion has a high win rate.”

Useful evidence about a population of games, filtered through a patch, rank, role, and selection context.

≠
Decision support

“This choice supplies the missing job in the plan we are considering.”

A narrower conclusion tied to an objective, visible composition, required capability, relevant conditions, and enemy pressure.

We do not promise a context-free answer. We promise to make the relevant context, reasoning, assumptions, and alternatives visible.

A tier list cannot explain whether another source of initiation adds anything to a team that already has reliable first contact. A champion label cannot show whether allied follow-through will exist after that champion reaches a target. Hermod is intended to perform that translation: from available evidence, to the job that matters, to the choices that can change it.

One need · different decisions

The useful answer changes with the person asking.

The same reasoning foundation can support several audiences, but only if it respects what each person controls, knows, and is trying to accomplish.

01Solo Queue player

What can I pick or do that helps this visible team?

Use role, familiarity, matchup, visible picks, and inferred team needs without pretending the team shares a declared strategy.

02Organized team

Can we execute the plan we agreed to build?

Use shared objectives, pathways, assignments, conditions, and coordination expectations to audit a deliberate plan.

03Coach or analyst

Why did the plan work or fail, and what should change?

Compare repeated evidence, dependencies, execution conditions, and alternatives instead of reducing review to blame.

04Meta analyst

What changed strategically when the environment changed?

Trace how patches and changing providers alter viable capabilities, exposures, pathways, and patterns of play.

The product should not force one level of reasoning onto every player.

It should begin with the decision they actually face, answer only what the available evidence supports, and show which additional context could change the conclusion.

The sociological problem

People need belonging, meaning, and a way to act together.

Competitive games are social worlds. They give us roles, shared stories, status, rivals, rituals, and a reason to care together. That is part of their beauty. It is also why strategic language can become tribal language. Certainty feels safer than ambiguity. A slogan travels faster than an explanation. Membership can become more important than whether a claim survives contact with the game.

Any community can lose sight of the people it was meant to serve. It becomes unhealthy when it asks for the surrender of individual judgment, makes doubt a form of disloyalty, turns doctrine into a shield against evidence, or invents an enemy to create unity. Growth is not worth that price.

We want the opposite. If there is a movement around Hermod, it should be a movement toward clearer reasons, inspectable assumptions, generous disagreement, and greater player agency. Its members should become harder to manipulate—including by us.

Our core values

Love for a community should look like useful restraint.

Love is not flattering people or claiming to speak for everyone. It is building with care, telling the truth about limits, and leaving people with more agency than they had before.

01

Honor the knowledge already here

Players, coaches, analysts, creators, and theorycrafters already understand more than any single model can contain. We are building a place where that knowledge can be named, compared, challenged, and carried forward.

02

Explain before you recommend

A useful answer shows its path: which action creates which effect, under what conditions, and how that effect helps a team perform a strategic job. A score without a reason asks for obedience. An explanation gives the player a choice.

03

Keep meaning in human hands

Software can derive relationships. It should not quietly decide what a community must call a strategy, what a team ought to value, or which tradeoff is morally correct. We derive structure and ask people to establish meaning.

04

Make every assumption revisable

Our defaults are conveniences, not sacred facts. If a champion profile, capability mapping, condition, or archetype does not match your experience, you should be able to inspect it, disagree with it, and eventually replace it with a better account.

05

Treat disagreement as useful evidence

Different teams can use the same champion for different purposes. Different players can draw different boundaries around an archetype. The goal is not to eliminate disagreement; it is to make the source of disagreement clear enough to learn from it.

06

Build independence, not dependence

Hermod succeeds when people become better at explaining, testing, and revising their own strategic ideas. We do not want a community that needs permission from our model. We want one that can use the model, criticize it, and outgrow parts of it.

The kind of movement we can support

Our ethical guardrails

These are promises we should be judged against, especially when growth makes shortcuts tempting.

Invitation, not conversion

You do not need to adopt our vocabulary to belong. Use what helps, change what does not, and bring the language your team already speaks.

Participation, not self-erasure

No player should have to surrender judgment or identity to a brand, leader, rank, or doctrine. The individual remains responsible for the decision.

No enemy required

We will not manufacture a villain out of Riot, professional analysts, casual players, ranked players, creators, or competing tools. Criticism should name a claim or system that can be examined—not a group that must be hated.

Specific work, not a promised utopia

We are solving concrete problems: tracing why a pick fits a plan, showing where a pathway is fragile, testing an assumption, and helping a team talk after the game.

Revisability, not doctrine

Evidence can change. Patches change. Players adapt. Community language evolves. A model that cannot be corrected eventually becomes a costume for certainty.

Service, not spectacle

The work should be useful when nobody is cheering: before a draft, during review, inside a difficult disagreement, and when the evidence tells us our favorite idea was wrong.

An invitation

Help us make strategy easier to see, discuss, and improve.

Bring the example our model misses. Challenge a capability mapping. Explain why two pathways that look structurally similar mean different things to your community. Show us where a condition is too broad. Define a strategy in your team’s own language. Make a prediction, test it, and keep the failed result.

We are not trying to replace the living culture of League with a finished taxonomy. We are trying to give that culture infrastructure: a place where insights can become inspectable, where disagreements can become precise, and where a player can move from “I know this feels wrong” to “Here is the job we cannot currently perform, and here is why.”

We want thoughtful participants, capable critics, generous teachers, and players who can define the meta together.
Community note

These principles are commitments, not a claim to speak for every League player. The community should judge Hermod by whether it remains useful, transparent, open to correction, and respectful of individual judgment as it grows.
